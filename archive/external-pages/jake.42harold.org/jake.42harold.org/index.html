Why do Minnesota drivers speed?

It’s a real shame the Office of Traffic Safety didn’t reach out to me first. Instead of spending a cool $150k to “devise” an online survey to find out why Minnesotans are lead foots, they could have paid me half of that and I would have told them it’s because we consistently make roads that encourage speeding by being way more generous with their geometries – wide lanes, wide shoulders, “clear zones”, too many lanes, the whole nine yards – than is necessary. Plus they cost us an arm and a leg, a debt that we’ll never be current on. Strong Towns has been beating this drum for over a decade now. It’s a funny feeling, to have stepped away from this conversation for so long only to come back to it and see that it hasn’t really gone anywhere.

Why not sidewalks?

For future reference, my humble take on why sidewalks aren’t a substitute for proper bike infrastructure, as lifted from this larger local Facebook “discussion”.

[…] Given our current state of affairs, sometimes sidewalks are the best option, depending on the age and abilities of the rider and the type/speed of traffic on the adjacent road. However, as was mentioned, sidewalk riding in the signed downtown area is illegal, per Minnesota statute. Outside of downtown, it is legal. But sidewalks are not substitutes for real bike infrastructure. They are called sideWALKS for a reason.

When riding on sidewalks, every driveway becomes a new conflict point between riders and drivers. At intersections, drivers are less accustomed to look for fast cross traffic coming at them from the sidewalk than they would be on the road, and bikers often fail to recognize this as well. And while our road conditions are nothing to write home about, they provide a better riding surface than sidewalks, plus they have the good fortune to go where people need to go and not suddenly end for no discernible reason.

In short, if you’re still on training wheels and learning to ride a bike, the sidewalk in front of your house is a great place to do it. But if you’re trying to get to school, work, or anywhere else that’s more than just a ride around the block, we need better options.

What’s it worth?

While Fergus Falls moves through the Kübler-Ross model (current forecast: anger turning to bargaining) as we deal with our latest local tragedy, some thoughts.

  1. Considering the shit storm that 2017 has been (and that’s not even mentioning the “T” word), let’s keep things in perspective (Of course, condolences to the Target employees losing their jobs).

  2. The act of Target leaving, and the disruption of old norms and rise of rumors and falsehoods and lies, will be more destructive than the departure itself. Can we rise above it?

  3. Toilet paper is toilet paper, no matter where you buy it.

Strong Towns has a timely post today. Titled “Big box stores are costing our cities far more than we ever imagined,” it’s a taste of their foray into data analysis of property tax rolls and the true cost of car-centric development patterns. While the city of Fergus Falls prepares to prostrate itself on Nicollet Mall, it’s worth taking a look into our local situation through the eyes of Strong Towns.

While my data model is far less sophisticated than that used by the Strong Towns team, it makes for pretty pictures (sort of) and having some objective numbers to look at always helps. so let’s go.

Target

Located midway between downtown and the interstate, Target sits on 8.6 acres of land. It’s a modest-sized store by Target standards. The taxable value of this parcel is abut $2.8 million; on a per acre basis (why this is important) that comes out to about $328k/acre. Based on conversations around town and various bits of commentary collected online, it also serves as an child/adult indoor play/escape space and provides a retail therapy environment, the benefits of which are not quantified in this model but are not to be ignored.

Shopko

One common refrain that I’ve heard is “How can Target close but Shopko remain in business?” Don’t put me on Marketplace, but Shopko is privately held, which sets up a different set of incentives for the company. Operating overhead is also less — their stores are smaller than the typical big box retailer, and, at least in the stores I’ve been in, are staffed at a much lower level. Still, it’s 2017, and they are in the retail business, so let’s not kid ourselves that things are going swimmingly here.

Here in Fergus Falls, the Shopko store is 0.67 acres and combined with its parking lot, clocks in at 1.86 acres. Taxable value of Shopko’s property is $692k; per acre it is actually more profitable for the city than Target at $372k. Plus, it’s located near downtown, which is a bonus for the mobility-challenged.

Walmart

For sake of comparison, I threw Walmart in the mix. Located on the far western edge of the city, Walmart spreads out over 18.35 acres. While it’s taxable value is the highest of the three retailers profiled here, at $5.5 million, taken on a per acre basis it’s actually the lowest contributor, coming in at just over $300k.

Continuing my unscientific sampling of social media chatter following the announcement from Target, it’s also worth noting that Walmart does not engender the type of love that Target does among Fergus Falls shoppers.

Downtown

All of this is to say that we already have a proven model for generating property taxes in Fergus Falls, and that any effort to collar a fleeing national retailer should be tempered by the realization that with some careful economic gardening, we could cultivate a diverse environment that is much more resilient and profitable than the monoculture offered by the national big box model.

This single block downtown (out of a modest three) offers more taxable value than Target and Shopko combined, and, taken on a per acre basis, is about five times more profitable for the city, and requires a fraction of the infrastructure (roads, sidewalks, pipes) to make it functional.

The chart below summarizes the areas depicted above.

I don’t doubt that losing Target is going to hurt the image of Fergus Falls, and the effects of the loss may very well ripple out and affect other stores and restaurants in the West Lincoln Avenue corridor as shoppers seek alternative destinations where a Target still remains. That being said, it seems strange for those who rag on Target Corporation for this decision to abandon a community will essentially do the same and roll over and say they will now drive 40 miles to frequent the next nearest store. This sort of boorish behavior is written into the DNA of corporations; we as citizens have our own moral compass to follow.

Maybe Fergus Falls can buck the trend and convince the powers that be to keep the store open a while longer, but the practice of betting on big development with oversized infrastructure needs, where control of the narrative rests largely on the whims of a balance sheet hundreds or thousands of miles away, seems like a bad gamble to me.

Mapping value

Hi, it’s been a while.

In my copious free time, I was able to gather property tax data for the city of Fergus Falls and parcel information and boundaries (thank you, Otter Tail County GIS!), and, inspired by the work of Strong Towns and Urban3, create a rudimentary take on their maps showing the taxable value per acre of the parcels throughout the city. (If you don’t know why this is an important metric, read this.)

Some highlights:

West Side

(Color key: Darker green + taller parcel = More value per acre; olive = city-owned (non-taxable); red = no taxable value; checkerboard pattern = church; crosshatch = school; orange = non-taxed railroad ROW)

This is our suburban strip on the west end of town by the interstate. Walmart is to the northwest of the exchange; Target is left of center south of the taller green box (a senior living complex). Fleet Farm is to the east of the short connector road near the center of the image and our dead mall is to the south of that. The most productive stars here are the aforementioned senior living complex and the walk-in (not) clinic. Contrast this with…

Downtown

Our main drag, Lincoln Avenue, runs through the center of the image and (for a couple of blocks, anyway) looks like Midtown Manhattan when it comes to taxable value per acre. Dead spaces to the north and south of Lincoln are parking lots and government buildings (City Hall, the police station, and the county courthouse). The skinny skyscrapers are the Victor Lundeen Company store and American Federal Bank, whose combined per-acre taxable value is over $20 million. Further development of the parking areas to the north and south could prove profitable for the city, but will the pretty drawings find a way to get turned into reality?

Hospital

Just south of downtown is the Lake Region Hospital healthcare complex. The three tallest parcels are, from top to bottom, the new clinic, a senior care facility, and a cancer treatment center. The main hospital complex, center, pays no property tax.

Traditional neighborhood

To the west of the hospital and southwest of downtown is a traditional neighborhood with a regular block pattern and smaller lot sizes (this is the best place to trick-or-treat in town, as it has an ideal ratio of doors per block). This is a useful neighborhood to compare against…

Woodland Heights

The newest entry into the city, this northern neighborhood was annexed in 2016, despite city officials not having a very good handle on future expenses related to roads, sewer, and other infrastructure that will be needed by this neighborhood in the next 5-10 years. Shown at the same scale as the traditional neighborhood, there are fewer parcels in a larger area and at a lower value per acre. Sustainable?

Hoot Lake Power Plant

The closing of this coal-fired power plant in 2020, while better for our lungs and environment, is what keeps the city bean counters up at night. Its tax shadow looms even larger than that of its smokestacks.

This is a fun data set to work with, and I hope to put it to good use in the upcoming weeks and months. Stay tuned?
