    Pirates of the Amazon Abandon Ship

    By Brad Stone
    December 5, 2008 1:27 pm December 5, 2008 1:27 pm

    Earlier this week, two students from the Piet Zwart Institute in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, made headlines by releasing a plug-in for the Firefox Web browser that made it easy for people who were browsing books, music and movies on Amazon.com to download the same products free through the Pirate Bay, the illicit BitTorrent site.

    Lawyers for Amazon.com promptly served the students’ Internet service provider with a take-down notice, and on Thursday the students complied and removed the tool.

    In an interesting twist, the students now say their project was a parody and an “experiment on interface design, information access and currently debated issues in media culture,” according to their Web site. One of the students, John, who did not give his last name, tried to explain further in an e-mail message.

    Amazon and the Pirate Bay “might look like opposites, but are actually quite similar in regards to the mainstream media content they provide,” he wrote. “Our project demonstrated this practically. So it’s a parody of any kind of media consumerism, whether corporate or subcultural.”

    Parodies are given a certain amount of protection under United States copyright law.

    John added: “The project struck quite some nerve; it surprised us that most people did not seem to get the humor and absurdity of it.”

    Florian Cramer, the course director of the Media Design program at Piet Zwart, wrote further about the project Friday on Nettime, a mailing list that covers issues of Internet culture and art. Mr. Cramer maintained that Pirates of the Amazon was legal, since the tool only provided simple links between two Web destinations, Amazon.com and the Pirate Bay. He also lamented the hostile reaction from the Internet community, saying that a majority of commentators failed to see the artistic nature of the experiment, and he worried that his students were being censored.

    “With the take down notice from Amazon.com, our students have been scared away from pursuing their art, research and learning in our institute,” Mr. Cramer wrote. “We do not want a culture in which students have to preemptively censor their study because their work confronts culture with controversial and challenging issues.”

    The students will be collecting further reactions to Pirates of the Amazon for their project. So, Bits readers, let’s give them a hand. What do you think of this experiment in free media, piracy and interface design?

    Shawn Smith December 5, 2008 · 4:40 pm

    First of all, this whole situation isn’t piracy, as Jesse (#7) pointed out.

    But let’s assume, for a second, that folks like “Ben (#8)” are actually correct, and this IS piracy. So what?

    Ben (#8) – You don’t “get it”, do you? According to your wide definition of theft, Corporate America (and corporate businesses, in general, worldwide) have been stealing from the public for quite a while now. In America, it’s become an accepted part of the corporate culture that it’s OK to steal from the public, for any number of reasons; “the public” is stupid and deserves to be stolen from, “the public” doesn’t understand how “the system” works, and deserves to be stolen from, as long as we don’t get caught it’s not illegal, etc. The reason why so many online “pirates” have been demonized over the last 15 years or so is because what the big corporations have done to smaller companies – and even their customers – for years now, is finally being done back to them. NOW it’s “unfair”?

    There’s a generation of people out there who are simply using basic capitalist economic theory, as it’s currently being practiced now, to get what they want. Their train of thought is simply, “If I can get it for free, why should I pay for it…” – the SAME attitude that the corporations have.

    Notice – I didn’t say that attitude is RIGHT. It just IS.

    If you want to punish the public thieves (users), ok. But you MUST also punish the corporate thieves too, each one punished at the level to which their “crime” has affected the public at large.

    Now whose “body” do you really think will be hanging from the proverbial yardarm when all is said and done, if justice is enforced appropriately?

    Sometimes, “pirates” are criminals; and sometimes, “pirates” are just people who are exploiting the system and the rules YOU set up against you.

    Remember, many folks who choose to support copyright privacy would prefer to pay what they see as a fair price, TO THE RIGHT INDIVIDUALS. The problem is, many industries (music, movies, etc.) have become FAR too top heavy in their management, by stealing from users (through overcharging) for too long. Now that the world economy is shrinking drastically, and their poor business model is being shown to be the overbloated sack of garbage that it is, they’re crying “Unfair” – because they’re being forced to pare back their businesses to a model where they’re going to have to gut a large portion of unnecessary management, if their business is going to survive.

    This is a problem, how?

    If the corporate pigs had known when to stop screwing over the public, and stealing from them, the current situation would never have happened in the first place.

    While I don’t pirate music, movies, books, etc. I personally, don’t have as much of a problem with those that do, BECAUSE of the way the current world marketplace is set up. No matter what you do, someone will ALWAYS steal SOME of your product (often, your own employees). It’s a fact of running a business.

    If you set up your business model to capture MOST of your potential business, however, while eliminating MOST reasons to steal from you, AND pricing your product at a fair and equitable rate, you will stop MOST theft — and usually make a MUCH bigger pile of $$$ than if you just stand there, stomping your little corporate feet, and yelling at the top of your little corporate lungs, “Unfair!”

    If Amazon WAS that kind of a company (which, generally, they are not) I’d have no trouble with this software add-on — IF this situation were piracy. As it isn’t piracy, and was simply an exploit of currently available technology, I also have no problem with it.

    I can see why Amazon would be scared, and not appreciate the add-on.
    But that doesn’t make it piracy.

    Cliff December 5, 2008 · 7:49 pm

    Alton wrote, “If someone buys it, the price is, by my definition, fair.”

    That is not true, if there is a monopoly. This is basic microeconomics.

    There are many monopolistic forces at work in the media industry:
    1. Copyright IS a monopoly: it grants the holder a temporary monopoly on a particular expression. Of course, one can argue whether 90 years is “temporary”.
    2. If a label has exclusive rights to a particular artist or album, then they have a de-facto monopoly on that: if someone wants to buy the album, they can ONLY get it from that label, and that is why CD prices have been so high – higher in fact than people feel they are actually worth.
    3. If distribution is limited to certain items that are being promoted, then there is a de-facto monopoly on the channels, resulting in limited access to or awareness of other items.

    Thus, there are many tangible reasons why prices are too high, and why there is great pressure from the public to circumvent the “system”, which has been monopolistic until the Internet. The Internet has broken the monopoly. In the absence of monopoly, prices should fall the an economic zero level: the point at which producing and selling media is no more profitable compared to other investments of the same funds and same level of risk.
